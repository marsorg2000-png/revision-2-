1. Unit 1: Political Systems Compared – Imperial Germany (1900) vs Weimar Republic (1919)

Topic 1

Direct Constitutional Comparison Table

Feature Imperial Germany (1900) Weimar Republic (1919)
Head of State Kaiser: Hereditary monarch, ruled for life, commanded armed forces, declared war, could propose laws and dismiss Chancellor. President: Democratically elected every 7 years, controlled army, held emergency decree powers under Article 48 (first President was Friedrich Ebert).
Head of Government Chancellor: Chosen personally by the Kaiser; answerable and responsible only to the Kaiser, not to parliament. Chancellor: Appointed by President from the Reichstag; required majority parliamentary support to govern.
Parliament Reichstag: Elected every 3 years; voted on laws and budget taxation, but could not initiate laws or dismiss the Chancellor. Reichstag: Elected every 4 years using Proportional Representation (PR); debated and voted on all legislation and taxes.
Electorate & Rights Men aged 25 and over had the vote. Bundesrat represented individual state princes. All men and women aged 20 and over; equal civil rights, free speech, freedom of religion, and right to assemble guaranteed.

2. The Kaiser's Rule, Industrial Pressures & The Devastating Impact of WW1

Topic 2
Kaiser’s Aims, Strengths & Internal Pressures
  • Industrial Boom: By 1914, Germany produced one-third of the world's electrical goods and surpassed Britain in steel production. Population surged from 49 to 65 million.
  • Militarism & Junkers: Elite Prussian noble landowners (Junkers) dominated the civil service and army officer corps; strong national pride in the military.
  • Domestic Friction: Kaiser Wilhelm II constantly clashed with Chancellors and resented the Reichstag.
  • Rise of Socialism: Rapid urbanization caused mushrooming trade unions (3 million members by 1914) and the growth of the SPD, organizing disruptive industrial strikes against autocratic rule.
The Triple Impact of World War One (1914–1918)
  • Economic Collapse: Industrial output plummeted to two-thirds of pre-war levels; national wealth was drained by four years of total war; national debt tripled.
  • Social Starvation: The British naval blockade caused catastrophic shortages (over 750,000 civilian deaths from malnutrition/hypothermia, e.g. the 1916–17 "Turnip Winter"). Workers suffered while industrialists earned state bonuses.
  • Political Revolution: Sailor mutinies at Kiel in October 1918 triggered workers' council revolts across cities; Kaiser Wilhelm II abdicated on 9 November 1918 and fled to the Netherlands. Ebert formed a provisional republic.

3. The Treaty of Versailles (1919) & The Hyperinflation Crisis of 1923

Topic 3

The Terms of the Treaty of Versailles (Signed 28 June 1919 – Hated as a 'Diktat')

1. Land Losses (10% / 13%)

Lost 10% of German land, 13% of population, and 26% of coal production. Alsace-Lorraine returned to France; Polish Corridor separated East Prussia.

2. Military Restrictions

Army capped at 100,000 volunteers; conscription banned; navy limited to 6 battleships; no submarines, tanks, or air force. Rhineland demilitarized.

3. Reparations (£6.6bn)

Total sum fixed at £6.6 billion ($33 billion) in 1921 to be paid to the Allies in annual financial and industrial installments over decades.

4. War Guilt (Article 231)

Germany forced to accept sole blame for causing the war. Deeply resented by Germans as an insult to national honor, branding politicians "November Criminals".

The 1923 Hyperinflation Catastrophe: Mechanism & Social Impact

The Cause & Mechanism:

Germany defaulted on timber/coal reparations in late 1922 → 60,000 French & Belgian troops occupied the industrial Ruhr basin in Jan 1923 → Weimar government sponsored passive resistance (general strike) → Government printed billions of paper banknotes to pay striking workers → Currency spiraled out of control: in Jan 1919 $1 = 9 marks; by Nov 1923 $1 = 2 billion marks (a loaf of bread cost 201 billion marks).

Wiped Out (Pensioners & Savers):

Middle-class families who had spent lifetimes building savings, insurance policies, or bonds saw life savings become completely worthless in weeks.

Severe Hardship (Workers):

Workers were paid twice daily and carried wages in wheelbarrows or laundry baskets before prices doubled again; starvation caused by wage lag.

Winners (Debtors & Big Business):

Business owners, farmers, and wealthy industrialists who had mortgages or loans paid off massive debts overnight with essentially worthless paper currency.

4. The Weimar Constitution, Political Uprisings & Stresemann’s Golden Era

Topic 4
Weimar Constitutional Balance
  • Strengths: Genuine democracy with universal suffrage (men & women 20+); proportional representation ensured fair voice for all votes; Bill of Rights guaranteed freedom of speech and religion.
  • Fatal Weakness 1 (PR): Dozens of splinter parties won seats, making a single-party majority impossible and forcing fragile coalitions that collapsed frequently.
  • Fatal Weakness 2 (Article 48): The President could suspend the constitution and rule by emergency decree without Reichstag consent in a "crisis", paving the road to dictatorship.
Three Early Threats from Extremists
  • Spartacist Uprising (Jan 1919): Left-wing communist revolt in Berlin led by Rosa Luxemburg and Karl Liebknecht. Ebert used the army and right-wing Freikorps to crush it; leaders murdered.
  • Kapp Putsch (March 1920): Right-wing Freikorps coup led by Wolfgang Kapp. Regular army refused to fire ("Troops do not fire on troops"); defeated by a city-wide general strike by Berlin workers.
  • Munich Putsch (Nov 1923): Hitler and Gen. Ludendorff hijacked a Munich beer hall attempting to march on Berlin; state police opened fire, killing 16 Nazis; Hitler arrested for treason.

Gustav Stresemann’s Recovery Policies (1924–1929: The Golden Years)

1. Currency Reform (1923)

Abolished the worthless mark and issued the temporary Rentenmark (backed by land), later the Reichsbank Reichsmark, killing hyperinflation.

2. Dawes & Young Plans

Dawes Plan (1924): $200m in US loans; Ruhr troops left. Young Plan (1929): Reduced reparations to £1.85bn over 59 years.

3. Foreign Treaties

Signed Locarno Pact (1925) respecting western borders; secured German entry into League of Nations (1926); signed Kellogg-Briand (1928).

4. Weimar Culture

Flourishing of avant-garde art (Otto Dix), Bauhaus architecture, cinema (Nosferatu, Metropolis), and cabaret. Traditionalists resented this as decadent.

5. Unit 2: The Great Depression (1929–1932) – Economic & Political Collapse

Topic 5
1. The Economic Mechanism

In October 1929, the Wall Street Crash struck the US. American banks urgently recalled short-term loans under the Dawes Plan within 90 days.

Without American capital, German businesses collapsed, factories shut, industrial output plunged by 40%, and tax receipts vanished.

2. Catastrophic Social Impact
  • Unemployment skyrocketed from 1.3 million (Sept 1929) to 6 million by 1932 (1 in 3 workers jobless).
  • Wages dropped by one-third; millions faced starvation, pawned belongings, and eviction into shantytowns.
  • Mass youth unemployment fueled hopelessness and hatred toward the Weimar Republic.
3. Political Paralysis & Brüning
  • The coalition collapsed over unemployment insurance funding; Chancellor Heinrich Brüning followed deflationary cuts.
  • Brüning cut unemployment benefits by 60%, earning the nickname the "Hunger Chancellor".
  • Unable to secure a Reichstag majority, Brüning bypassed parliament using President Hindenburg's Article 48 emergency decrees, destroying democracy before Hitler took office.

6. The Growth & Electoral Breakthrough of the Nazi Party (1929–1932)

Topic 6
Hitler’s Oratory & Image

Charismatic speeches tapped into national despair. In Mein Kampf, he offered scapegoats: Jews, communists, and the "November Criminals", promising to tear up the Versailles Treaty.

Goebbels’ Propaganda

Masterminded mass rallies, modern planes ("Hitler Over Germany"), loudspeakers, 120 party newspapers, and simple slogans tailored to specific audience fears.

Role of the SA (Stormtroopers)

400,000 brownshirts provided hot soup, uniforms, and purpose to jobless young men while physically beating up communists, projecting an illusion of disciplined order amid chaotic Weimar street fighting.

Broad Electoral Appeal

Promised higher crop prices to farmers, protection from big department stores to shopkeepers, anti-communist security to industrialists, and jobs to workers.

7. Hitler’s Consolidation of Total Power & Dictatorship (1930–1934)

Topic 7

Between 1930 and 1934, Hitler transformed Germany from a parliamentary democracy into a totalitarian one-party dictatorship via backroom political maneuvering, emergency decrees, terror, and legislative purges.

Date & Event How It Helped Hitler Consolidate Total Power
1930–1932 Elections Nazis surged from 12 seats (1928) to 107 seats (1930), and became the largest party with 230 seats (37.3%) in July 1932. Brüning, Papen, and Schleicher all failed to lead stable coalitions.
30 January 1933: Appointment as Chancellor Franz von Papen convinced President Hindenburg to appoint Hitler Chancellor in a coalition cabinet, falsely boasting: "Within two months we will have pushed Hitler so far into a corner that he'll squeak!" with only 3 Nazis in cabinet.
27 Feb 1933: Reichstag Fire Dutch communist Marinus van der Lubbe was blamed. Hitler persuaded Hindenburg to sign the Reichstag Fire Decree, suspending civil liberties, freedom of the press, and legalizing the arrest of 4,000 communist leaders.
24 March 1933: The Enabling Act Passed 444 votes to 94 in the Kroll Opera House while SA armed guards intimidated deputies (communists banned, Centre Party bribed). Allowed Hitler to pass laws without the Reichstag or President for 4 years, ending democracy.
May–July 1933: Gleichschaltung (Coordination) In May, independent trade unions were banned and replaced by the Nazi German Labour Front (DAF). On 14 July 1933, the Law Against the Establishment of Parties made the NSDAP the sole legal party in Germany.
30 June 1934: Night of the Long Knives Hitler used Himmler's black-shirt SS to murder SA leader Ernst Röhm and up to 200 political rivals. Röhm wanted the 3-million-strong SA to absorb the regular army and lead a socialist "second revolution". The purge won Hitler the army's loyalty.
August 1934: Death of Hindenburg & Army Oath President Hindenburg died. Hitler merged the offices of President and Chancellor into one supreme title: Der Führer. Every soldier swore a sacred personal oath of unconditional obedience to Adolf Hitler.

8. Unit 3: Nazi Economic Policies, Reducing Unemployment & Living Standards

Topic 8
Methods Used to Tackle Unemployment
  • Public Works Projects: Massive state infrastructure spending built 2,000 miles of Autobahns (motorways), hospitals, schools, and railways.
  • National Labour Service (RAD): Compulsory 6-month manual labor service for men aged 19–25 (extended to women in 1939) planting forests and digging drainage ditches for pocket money.
  • Rearmament: Open military expansion after 1935 grew the army to 900,000; military spending consumed two-thirds of government expenditure by 1936.
  • "Invisible Unemployment": Hundreds of thousands were removed from official unemployment counts: married women forced out of jobs, Jews stripped of employment, political prisoners in camps, and military conscripts.
Impact of Nazi Rule on Living Standards
  • Big Business: Thrived under the ban on strikes and trade unions; earned record profits from state armaments contracts (e.g. Krupp, IG Farben).
  • Small Businesses: Hurt by Nazi focus on heavy rearmament and department store competition; over 300,000 small firms closed.
  • Farmers: Benefited initially from cleared debts and higher tariffs (incomes rose 41% by 1936), but later squeezed by strict state food price caps and agricultural labor shortages.
  • Industrial Workers: Achieved guaranteed full employment, but lost right to strike or negotiate wages. Working hours increased from 43 to 49 hours per week by 1939. Compensated with subsidized KdF (Strength Through Joy) cruises, cinema tickets, and Beauty of Labour (SdA) canteen improvements.

9. Social Control & Daily Life: Women, Youth, Churches & The Police State

Topic 9
Women in Nazi Germany
  • Ideology: Kinder, Küche, Kirche (Children, Kitchen, Church); natural role was mother and homemaker; banned from judicial and senior civil service roles.
  • Policies: Marriage loans up to 1,000 marks (repayment reduced by 25% per child born); the Mother’s Cross awarded annually on Hitler's mother's birthday (Bronze 4, Silver 6, Gold 8 children); abortion criminalized.
  • Reality & War Contradiction: As rearmament surged and men went to war, female employment actually grew from 4.2 million to 6.2 million in factories.
Youth & Education
  • Nazified Schools: PE time tripled (15% of timetable); History focused on Versailles betrayal and German victories; Biology taught Nazi racial purity (Eugenics); teachers forced to join the Nazi Teachers' League.
  • Youth Movements: Hitler Youth (HJ) drilled boys in physical toughness, marching, map-reading, and rifle-shooting; BDM (League of German Girls) prepared girls for motherhood and domesticity; made compulsory in 1939 (membership reached 7.3m).
Churches: Cooperation & Conflict
  • Catholic Church: Signed the 1933 Concordat with Pope Pius XI (agreeing non-interference in exchange for keeping Catholic schools); Hitler broke terms, shutting youth clubs and arresting priests.
  • Protestant Churches: Set up the pro-Nazi Reich Church led by Ludwig Müller; opposed by Pastor Martin Niemöller and Dietrich Bonhoeffer, who formed the anti-Nazi Confessional Church and were sent to concentration camps.
Police State Terror & Propaganda
  • The SS (Schutzstaffel): Led by Heinrich Himmler; ruthless elite protection squads and racial purity overseers who ran the concentration camps (Dachau founded 1933).
  • The Gestapo: Secret state police that arrested and tortured suspects without trials or search warrants, heavily aided by civilian informers.
  • Propaganda & Censorship: Joseph Goebbels enforced total control over media: cheap radios (People's Receiver), street loudspeakers, book burnings (May 1933), banning American jazz music, and labeling modern art "degenerate".

10. Persecution of Minorities & The Escalation to the Holocaust

Topic 10
Persecution of Other Minority Groups
  • Disabled People: Over 350,000 forcibly sterilized under the 1933 Sterilization Law; from 1939, over 70,000 mentally and physically disabled citizens were secretly gassed under the T4 Euthanasia program.
  • Romani People (Gypsies): Deemed racially impure; over 500,000 murdered in concentration and death camps across Europe.
  • Homosexuals: Approximately 15,000 sent to concentration camps (wearing pink triangles) to undergo forced hard labor.
  • Jehovah’s Witnesses & "Asocials": Thousands imprisoned for refusing to swear the military oath to Hitler or perform military service.
The Escalation of Anti-Semitic Persecution
  • 1933: 1-day nationwide boycott of Jewish shops; Jews barred from civil service, teaching, and legal professions.
  • 1935 Nuremberg Laws: Stripped Jews of German citizenship; banned marriage and sexual relationships between Jews and Aryans.
  • 1938 Kristallnacht (9–10 Nov): State-sponsored pogrom; 1,000 synagogues burned, 7,000 Jewish businesses trashed, 91 Jews murdered, and 30,000 sent to camps. Jewish community fined 1 billion marks.
  • 1939–1941 Ghettos & Einsatzgruppen: Invasion of Poland and USSR trapped millions in sealed ghettos (Warsaw); SS death squads (Einsatzgruppen) carried out mass machine-gun executions.
  • 1942 Wannsee Conference & Final Solution: Senior Nazi officials coordinated industrial genocide using extermination death camps (Auschwitz-Birkenau, Treblinka); murdered 6 million Jewish people.

11. World War Two Realities on the Home Front & Internal Opposition

Topic 11
Wartime Home Front Realities (1939–1945)
  • Initial Euphoria (1939–1941): Early Blitzkrieg victories across Poland, France, and the West brought seized foreign food and goods, keeping civilian morale high.
  • Total War Turning Point (1942–1943): Catastrophic defeat at Stalingrad (Feb 1943) prompted Goebbels' "Total War" speech. Civilian life was militarized: non-essential shops closed and 60-hour workweeks mandated.
  • Allied Bombing: Round-the-clock bombing by the RAF and USAAF devastated cities (e.g. Dresden, Hamburg firestorms), destroying 2 million homes and killing 350,000 civilians. Severe rationing and refugees broke public morale.
Internal Opposition & Resistance Movements
  • The July Bomb Plot (20 July 1944): Army officers led by Colonel Claus von Stauffenberg planted a briefcase bomb inside Hitler's Rastenburg headquarters (Operation Valkyrie). Hitler survived; 5,000 plotters were executed.
  • The White Rose Group: Munich University students led by siblings Hans and Sophie Scholl distributed anti-Nazi and anti-war leaflets; arrested by Gestapo and beheaded in 1943.
  • Youth Rebellion: Edelweiss Pirates beat up Hitler Youth patrols and sheltered army deserters; Swing Youth listened to banned American jazz music and danced in defiance of Nazi rules.
  • Religious Resistance: Catholic Bishop von Galen publicly preached against the T4 euthanasia program, forcing its temporary pause; Protestant pastor Dietrich Bonhoeffer helped Jews escape and joined plots against Hitler before his execution in 1945.
