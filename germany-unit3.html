Unit 3: Nazi Germany — Dictatorship in Action & Society

Active View:

Nazi Economic Policies & Unemployment Solutions

Section 1

Overview: Employment levels and improving the economy were core parts of Nazi election promises. They also wanted to improve international standing and reputation so they could seem like they were benefiting the German people, growing the economy, and creating jobs.

Public Works

Lots of government investment into public projects like building autobahns. Provided jobs for unemployed, improved the economy, and developed the country. 2,000 miles of autobahns, extended railways, new canals, and bridges.

National Labour Service (RAD)

1935: 19-25 year old men given compulsory work. Extended to women in 1939. Provided accommodation in barracks so location was less important, allowing employment where work was rather than where workers lived.

Rearmament

Creating a bigger army was core to Hitler's policies. 2/3 of government spending went to the military by 1936. Created millions of factory jobs and boosted national pride.

Invisible Unemployment

Women and Jewish people not counted, making employment levels look better. Conscription introduced in 1935 (18-25 year olds did 2 years), counting as employed and expanding armed forces from 100,000 to 1.4 million.

Impact of Economic/Job Policies on the German People

Big Businesses

✓ Trade unions banned so workers had fewer rights; companies could work people longer and cut sick pay.
✓ Kept wages low.
✓ Firms like Daimler-Benz benefited from rearmament contracts.

Small Businesses

✗ Government focus on rearmament meant consumer goods producers lost out.
✗ Over 300,000 small businesses lost out and had to close.

Farmers

✓ Initially benefited: debts cancelled, food prices increased (incomes up 41% 1933-36).
✗ Strict price controls, limited growth, worker shortages, and low farm tech investment.

Workers

✓ By 1939 only 0.14% male unemployment; armament workers paid more.
✗ Agriculture and consumer goods workers paid less; longer working hours.

German Labour Front (DAF)
  • All trade unions disbanded and incorporated into the DAF (less protection for workers).
  • Organized training for 2.5 million workers to retrain where needed.
  • Employers in control; no strikes or protests allowed.
  • Refusing assigned jobs meant forced labour camp detention (no voluntary unemployment).
Strength Through Joy (KdF)
  • Improved leisure opportunities and cheap sports.
  • Over 10 million took subsidized holidays.
  • 1938 Volkswagen savings scheme let workers buy a car through a fund, but no worker ever gained a car and it was scrapped in 1939 for military production.
Beauty of Labour (SdA)
  • Aimed to improve working conditions.
  • Provided low-cost food and new washing facilities in factories.
  • Workers still lacked legal rights or negotiating power.

Impact of the Second World War on the German People

Section 2
Morale
  • Lack of preparation at war start with shortages in fuel, oil, and steel, but initial morale was generally good.
  • Germany winning early on: invaded USSR in 1941, conquered most of Europe by end of 1942.
  • 1943 turning point: military situation worsened so morale fell.
  • News suppressed by press but seeped in via soldiers on leave and secret BBC broadcasts, leading some to stay away from Nazi rallies or refuse the Hitler salute.
Labour Shortages
  • 13 million men drafted into the armed forces, creating mass labor shortages.
  • Addressed by employing women and concentration camp prisoners as forced labor.
  • Factories pivoted entirely to munitions; workers forced into extra shifts, holidays banned, minimum 60-hour work weeks.
Rationing
  • Introduced at war start due to farm labor shortages.
  • Clothing and shoe shortages led Nazi officials to begin confiscating footwear.
  • Diet restricted to bread, potatoes, and vegetables; reasonable until mid-1944, then worsening and dire by 1945.
Allied Bombing
  • Despite Hitler's promises that German cities would never be bombed, Allied air raids escalated.
  • 1943 civilian targets hit: 350,000 killed, 780,000 injured, and 2 million homes destroyed.
Refugees (1945)

By 1945, Allied forces advancing from the west and Soviet forces from the east produced massive refugee waves fleeing westward, straining already disastrous conditions in West Germany.

Nazi Policies on Women (Kinder, Küche, Kirche)

Section 3

Philosophy: Nazi policy toward women was influenced by beliefs that men were naturally suited for leadership and national defense, while women were "nurturing" homemakers with no public role. Their primary duty was addressing Germany's falling birth rate by bearing strong, racially pure children.

Political Impact on Women
  • Exclusion from Government: Banned from the Reichstag and unrepresented.
  • Judicial Bans: Banned from serving as judges or lawyers in 1936.
  • Loss of Influence: Promoted the idea that women should play no role in running or defending the state.
Economic Impact on Women
  • Marriage Loans: Up to 1,000 marks (half-year pay); required quitting work. Repayment cut by 25% per child; 4 children meant zero repayment.
  • State Sacks: Female professionals (doctors, teachers, civil servants) sacked.
  • Education Quotas: Higher education capped at 10%.
  • Reality vs Policy: Employed women rose from 4.2m to 6.2m; over 6m worked in agriculture during WWII.
Social, Cultural & Lifestyle
  • Family: Abortions restricted; childlessness became grounds for divorce; Mother's Cross medals awarded.
  • Domestic Training: State-run Women's Enterprises trained women in domestic duties.
  • Behavior & Dress: Slimming, smoking, late nights, trousers, and hair dye frowned upon; restaurants banned female smoking.

Education & Youth Control

Section 4
Schools & Curriculum
  • History: German victories & Treaty of Versailles grievances.
  • Geography: Territories for future conquest.
  • Science & Maths: Weapon calculations and discriminatory statistics (e.g., cost of disabled people).
  • PE: Time trebled to create the best soldiers.
  • Race Studies: Eugenics. Teachers required to teach Nazi ideology and join the German Teachers' League.
Youth Groups & Compulsion
  • Timeline: Hitler Youth created in 1922; all other youth groups banned in 1933; 1936 Law gave Hitler Youth 'equal status' to school/home; membership made compulsory in 1939.
  • Boys: Little Fellows (6-10), Young Folk (10-14), Hitler Youth (14-18) — trained as future soldiers and heads of families.
  • Girls: Young Girls (10-14), League of German Girls (14-17) — trained to be "good women".
  • Youth Opposition: Refusing to join, listening to banned jazz (black origins), and groups like "roving dudes" and "Edelweiss Pirates" who camped and mocked Nazis.

The Churches in Nazi Germany (Protestant & Catholic)

Section 5

Hitler hated Christianity as its teachings of love and tolerance clashed with Nazi ideology and the idea of Hitler as supreme leader. With 40m Protestants and 22m Catholics (32%), the Church was immensely popular, forcing Hitler to tread carefully with gradual control, the Concordat, and the Reich Church.

Methods of Control
  • Concordat: Agreement with the Pope ensuring church independence from politics in exchange for loyalty oaths from bishops.
  • Closing Church Groups: Youth groups forced to merge with Hitler Youth by 1936.
  • Reich Church: Protestant unification under Bishop Ludwig Müller ("with the swastika on our chests and the cross in our hearts").
  • Intimidation: Arresting critical priests; Jehovah's Witnesses faced 1/3 mortality in camps.
Church Opposition
  • Confessional Church: Led by Martin Niemöller (6,000 pastors refusing Nazi allegiance).
  • Bishop von Galen: Highly popular; criticized racial policies and successfully halted the 1941 euthanasia program.
  • Dietrich Bonhoeffer: Denounced Nazism as anti-Christian from 1933, helped Jews escape, and was executed in 1945.
Reasons for Lack of Opposition
  • Many Germans actively supported Nazis or sat in the middle.
  • Nazis agreed with traditional moral and family values.
  • Church leaders wanted to protect religious institutions.
  • Widespread fear of atheistic communism.

Persecution of Minorities & "Socially Useless" Groups

Section 6
Target Group When & How Persecuted Reasoning / Ideology
Those Who Wouldn't Work Rounded up in 1933; 500,000 sent to concentration camps. Deemed "socially useless".
The Disabled 350,000 with genetic diseases forcibly sterilized. Euthanasia program (1939): 52,000 children and 71,000 adults killed by 1941. Regarded as a financial and genetic burden the government shouldn't provide for.
Homosexuals 15,000 sent to concentration camps; many castrated. Violated traditional family values.
Political Enemies Put in concentration camps without trial. Demanded everyone follow Hitler unconditionally.
Non-Aryans (Roma & Black Germans) 385 Black Germans sterilized; marriage bans; 25,000 of 30,000 Roma Germans and 500,000 Roma total killed. True Germans were Aryans; necessity of keeping the master race pure.

Timeline & Impact: Persecution of Jewish People

Section 7
1933: Boycotts & Bans

Shops boycotted, banned from state jobs, and children segregated in schools.

1935: Nuremberg Laws

Intermarriage banned, stripped of German citizenship, voting rights denied.

1936: Berlin Olympics

Outward signs of persecution temporarily removed in Berlin for PR.

1938: Total Exclusion

Banned from radios, bikes, businesses, cinemas, and state schools.

Nov 1938: Kristallnacht

Violent pogrom destroying synagogues and homes. 91+ killed, thousands sent to camps.

1939: Poland Invasion

3.5m more Jews under control, confined to overcrowded Polish ghettos.

1941: USSR Invasion

Einsatzgruppen death squads executed Jews by shooting.

Jan 1942: Wannsee

Planned the "Final Solution", establishing death camps where 6m Jews were murdered.

Impact of Persecution on Jewish People

Work

Boycotted and attacked; struggled to make money, leading to poverty and exclusion.

Leisure

Denied cinemas, radios, bikes, etc., completely excluded from society.

Education

Barred from state schools and universities, severely limiting future jobs.

Citizenship

Citizenship removed, losing access to state provisions and benefits.

Safety

Regularly attacked and placed in constant physical danger.

Fear and Repression — The Police State

Section 8

The Nazis needed to bring the country under control through fear. Originally, they kept concentration camps secret due to unpopularity, but eventually, everyone knew, acting as a powerful deterrent against rebellion.

Concentration Camps

Political opponents and resisters were imprisoned in brutal conditions.

Heinrich Himmler & SS

Himmler controlled all police forces with absolute loyalty. SS (1925) evolved from bodyguards to a feared violent force; the SD could arrest anyone, including Nazi members.

Police

Ignored crimes committed by Nazis; top police jobs were held by Nazis.

The Gestapo

Secret police that spied on citizens and could arrest anyone for any reason without trial.

"Winning People Over" — Propaganda and Control

Section 9

The flip side of fear was using propaganda to persuade the German people of Nazi party policies.

Joseph Goebbels

Made Minister of Enlightenment and Propaganda, coordinating all media and public messaging.

Newspapers

Published only positive stories about Nazis and negative stories about Jewish people.

Mass Rallies

Spectacular annual events celebrating Hitler's greatness in specially built arenas.

Radio Expansion

Controlled radio stations; loudspeakers installed in streets; increased radio ownership during the 1930s, surpassing American levels.

Books, Theatres & Music

Forced to produce content praising Hitler and Nazi ideology.

Effects of the Nazis on Art and Culture

Section 10
Cinema

Goebbels read and approved all film scripts. Nazi supporters like Hugenberg owned film studios, giving influence over production. Hitler newsreels shown before every film.

Music

Promoted German/Austrian classical music (Bach, Mozart, Wagner). Weimar era music, Jewish composers (Mahler, Mendelssohn), and jazz (due to black origins) were banned.

Theatre

The Militant League for German Culture protested modern plays. Kurt Weill's The Threepenny Opera targeted. Plays required to focus on German history.

Literature

Goebbels created a list of banned books. Mein Kampf became the best-seller. Remarque's 'All Quiet on the Western Front' banned.

Art

Hitler hated modern art, deeming it degenerate. In 1936, the Nazis burned 5,000 paintings considered unacceptable.

Opposition to the Nazis

Section 11
The Army (July Bomb Plot)

Became critical in 1943 as war went badly (failed attempts in 1943). Count von Stauffenberg attempted to kill Hitler with a suitcase bomb in Operation Valkyrie, but the suitcase was moved. 4 killed, Hitler sustained minor injuries. Stauffenberg arrested, accomplice Beck committed suicide, 5,746 executed.

The White Rose

Student resistance led by Hans and Sophie Scholl and Professor Kurt Huber at Munich University. Spread anti-Nazi messages and opposed the war. Reported by a janitor to the Gestapo and hanged.

Edelweiss Pirates

Working-class movement that beat up Hitler Youth members, met in mixed-gender groups, and distributed Allied leaflets during the war. Caught by the Gestapo and publicly hanged.

The Church

The only organization with its own ideology. Confessional Church (Martin Niemöller) refused Nazi allegiance (6,000 pastors by 1934). Bishop von Galen criticized racial policies and euthanasia (halting it temporarily). Dietrich Bonhoeffer opposed Nazism as anti-Christian, helped Jews escape, executed in 1945.

Swing Youth

Middle-class teenagers who listened to jazz and went clubbing. Their activities threatened the regime, which shut down bars associated with them.
