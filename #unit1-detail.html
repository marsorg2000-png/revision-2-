Ideological Differences: Communism vs Capitalism

At its most simple, the cold war was a war between two ideologies (ways of thinking); communism and capitalism. Communists mainly believed that everyone should be equal and capitalists mainly believed that everyone should be free to make their own decisions.

Communism
  • Controlled Economy: The government has a lot of power over how goods and resources are produced, distributed, and used in society.
  • Government Ownership: Government owns factories, farms, and means of production to decide what gets produced, how much, and take a share of profits.
  • Central Planning: Government makes all big decisions about what goods and services are needed with detailed plans.
  • Allocation of Resources: Government assigns labor and resources to industries or regions, reducing individual freedom.
  • Government: One-party government (candidates are all from the same party).
  • Standard of Living: Lower average standard of living, but more equal.
Capitalism
  • Free Economy: Government has no control over prices or production of goods.
  • Free Allocation: People choose jobs they apply for; companies hire freely.
  • Free Market: Businesses and individuals decide what to produce based on supply and demand.
  • Market Allocation: Market allocates resources; if there's a gap in the market people will fill it.
  • Government: Multiple political parties chosen by the people in elections.
  • Standard of Living: Higher average standard of living, but greater inequality.

Why was there mistrust between USA and USSR before WW2?

Ideological differences: Worried that if the other ideology took over they wouldn't be able to control the spread.
First World War: Russia withdrew in late 1917 and the allies (Britain and France) felt abandoned.
Propaganda: The “Red Scare” – the West suggesting that communism was a threat to them.
Russian Civil War: Western countries supported the Whites against the Reds (communism).
Stalin’s regime: Many people mistrusted Stalin because of his brutality and leadership style.
Nazi-Soviet pact: Nazis and USSR signed a non-aggression pact in 1939 – West thought this was a betrayal.

2. Yalta and Potsdam Conferences

Topic 2

Towards the end of the Second World War, victory was looking likely for the US and USSR against Nazi Germany. The leaders of the US, USSR and Britain decided to sit down to discuss what would happen when the war ended at the Yalta conference. They then later sat down once the war had already ended with Germany to properly set out a plan at the Potsdam conference.

Yalta Conference

The West’s Aims:
  • East-West co-operation should continue.
  • Germany should be rebuilt as an independent, democratic country.
  • Countries in Eastern Europe should have the right to self-determination and be free from outside influence.
  • The United Nations should be formed to help avoid conflicts.
  • There should be economic co-operation through the World Bank and the International Monetary Fund (IMF).
The USSR’s Aims:
  • East-West co-operation should continue.
  • Spheres of influence should be created to guarantee security.
  • Germany should remain weak.
  • German industry should pay for the rebuilding of the USSR.
  • The World Bank and the IMF should have no authority over the USSR, but the USSR would be prepared to work within the United Nations (UN).
Churchill (British leader): Aware of Stalin’s aims and wanted to protect British interests. Wanted a close relationship with the USA. Struggled to understand Stalin’s point of view. Naturally mistrustful of Stalin as a strong anti-communist.
FDR (US leader): Committed to working with the USSR and got on well with Stalin. Believed that only a capitalist Europe could prevent a future war. Some argue he misunderstood Stalin’s aims.
Stalin (USSR leader): Wanted to ensure total devastation suffered by USSR could never happen again. Believed creating a sphere of influence was the best security. Recognized need for cooperation with West.
Key Yalta Agreements:

Germany to be divided into 4. Free elections for Eastern Europe. UN to be formed to stop another war.

Potsdam Conference

Reasons for relationship deteriorating between conferences: Should Germany pay reparations? (Stalin yes, Truman and Attlee disagreed). Self-determination in Eastern Europe (Stalin wanted soviet influence, West wanted independence). Red army was occupying most of Eastern Europe. No longer a common enemy in Germany. Change of leaders meant positive relationship from Yalta wasn't continued.

Attlee (British leader)

Focused on domestic plans, e.g., welfare state. Worried about Soviet expansion. Wanted a secure Germany. Saw Britain as weaker and needing American friendship.

Truman (US leader)

Wanted self-determination for European countries. Distrusted Stalin and his intentions. Thought America had the upper hand because of the atomic bomb.

Stalin (USSR leader)

Saw America as a rival and wanted security for the USSR. Thought he was in a powerful position with the Red Army occupying Eastern Europe. Distrusted America now it had the atomic bomb.

Key Potsdam Agreements:
  • The division of Germany was confirmed into 4 zones.
  • The Nazi Party was banned, and its leaders were put on trial.
  • Germans living in Poland, Hungary, and Czechoslovakia were to be returned to Germany.
  • Poland was to lose some territory to the USSR.

Impact of the Atomic bomb: Ended the war in Pacific – no need for an alliance. Truman hadn’t told Stalin, causing deep distrust.

3. Soviet Expansionism & Ideological Policies

Topic 3

Soviet Satellite States Takeover in Eastern Europe

The USSR wanted to expand their influence in the East. According to agreements at Potsdam they would allow “self-determination” which meant countries could decide for themselves how they wanted to be governed.

Country & Year Takeover Details
Albania (1945) The Communists took power after the war without opposition.
Bulgaria (1945) A left-wing coalition gained power in 1945; the Communists then executed the leaders of all the other parties.
Poland (1947) A coalition government took power in 1945, but the Communists forced the non-Communist leaders into exile.
Hungary (1947) Hungary was invaded by Russians, and in 1945 Allies agreed Russian troops stay. Rakosi got control of police and arrested opponents, setting up a brutal secret police unit (AVH). Rakosi soon had complete control.
Romania (1945–1947) A left-wing coalition was elected in 1945; the Communists gradually took over control.
Czechoslovakia (1945–1948) A left-wing coalition was elected in 1945. In 1948, the Communists banned all other parties and killed their leaders.
East Germany (1949) The Russians turned their zone of Germany into the German Democratic Republic in 1949.

Western Response to Soviet Expansionism

Long Telegram (February 1946)

800-word telegram from US embassy in Moscow about developments within the USSR, written by Ambassador George Kennan to Truman.

  • Soviets wanted to spread influence as widely as possible.
  • The USSR saw the USA as its enemy.
  • Any attempt at co-operation between the USA and USSR would fail. This confirmed Truman’s fears.
Iron Curtain (Winston Churchill)
  • The USSR was attempting to spread its influence across the rest of Europe and increase its power.
  • An invisible line had split Europe in two, between the East and the West. He called the line the Iron Curtain.

Ideological Policies (US vs USSR)

Both communists and capitalists didn’t want the opposite ideology to expand because they were scared it would take over in their own country. This meant they launched policies to stop this from happening.

Marshall Plan (US - 1947)

European Recovery Program – an example of the Truman Doctrine in action.

  • Purpose: Aid economic recovery in Europe to prevent communism; support containment; create a market for American goods.
  • Implementation: $13.15 billion divided among countries willing to accept aid. Aid provided as money/resources (must be purchased from US suppliers). UK got $3.2bn, France $2.7bn, Italy $1.5bn.
  • Results: Western economies recovered, living standards rose, US economy benefited. Communism lost popularity in West Europe. East/West division entrenched as Stalin forbade Eastern Bloc nations from accepting aid. Yugoslavia accepted it (seen as a betrayal by Stalin).
Truman Doctrine (US - March 1947)

American policy towards communism outlined by President Truman in a speech to Congress.

  • Communism posed a serious threat to the USA and world.
  • The USA would support any country under threat from communism.
  • Focus on 'containing communism' within existing countries. Response to situation in Greece and Turkey.
Cominform (USSR)

Stalin’s version of the Truman Doctrine. Saw Truman Doctrine as a threat to communism; brought communist countries together in Moscow to ensure uniformity in laws and ideology.

Comecon (USSR - 1949)

Stalin’s version of the Marshall Plan to provide financial aid and economic integration for Eastern European countries.

4. Berlin Blockade and Airlift (1948–1949)

Topic 4
Causes
  • West wanted to rebuild Germany and Stalin wanted it to remain weak.
  • In March 1948, Western Allies unified their sectors and introduced a new currency (Deutsche Mark). Stalin followed suit in the East.
  • Stalin wanted the West out of Berlin so was trying to make their life difficult.
The Blockade
  • From April 1948, a 'mini-blockade' included military supply route blockages and traffic restrictions.
  • On 24 June 1948, Stalin launched a full blockade, cutting transport links and electricity to West Berlin, trapping 2.5 million inhabitants without essential supplies.
Western Response
  • USA and UK initiated an airlift on 26 June 1948. At its peak, planes arrived every three minutes, delivering 4,000 tonnes daily.
  • On 15 April 1949, the 'Easter Parade' delivered nearly 12,000 tonnes of coal.
  • On 12 May 1949, Stalin ended the blockade.

5. Spread of Communism in Asia: China & The Korean War

Topic 5

The Communist Revolution in China (1949)

Civil War led to the People’s Republic of China on 1 October 1949 under a communist government.

Relationship with Stalin:

Stalin recognized communists as rightful government. Treaty of Friendship (Feb 1950) provided $300m in aid (95% repayable, spent on Soviet goods), 8,000 Chinese students to study tech, 20,000 Soviet experts sent (looking after Soviet interests), and mining rights in Xinjiang territory.

Relationship with West:

The West didn't recognize the new government. Seen as a failure of containment. September 1949 NSC-68 allowed major US military buildup.

The Korean War (1950–1953)

1. Tensions

US and USSR divided Korea at 38th Parallel at Potsdam. North: Kim Il Sung (Soviet-trained). South: Syngman Rhee (US-backed anti-communist). Rhee wanted US help to overthrow Kim; Kim sent raiding parties across border.

2. Events

1949: Kim asked Stalin for invasion support due to 7,500 US troops threat. 1950: Stalin supplied weapons (no troops). June: North invaded South. US asked UN for ceasefire; USSR was boycotting so couldn't veto. UN sent troops (mostly US). September: MacArthur drove North back to 38th parallel. October: Chinese troops pushed forces down to Seoul. April 1951: MacArthur called for nuclear bomb, was sacked. June: Stalemate. July 1953: Ceasefire ended war at original border.

3. Consequences

Showed US willingness to go to war. Showed UN power (causing Soviet mistrust). First proxy war. Dismissal of MacArthur showed USA and USSR were keen to avoid direct conflict.

6. NATO vs The Warsaw Pact

Topic 6
Feature NATO The Warsaw Pact
Formed April 1949. Created in response to the Berlin Blockade and Airlift. 1955, created in response to West Germany being allowed to join NATO.
Membership 12 original members: USA, Canada, Britain, France, Denmark, Italy, Norway, Belgium, Netherlands, Portugal, Luxembourg, Iceland. West Germany joined 1955. 8 members: USSR, Bulgaria, Romania, Albania, East Germany, Poland, Hungary, Czechoslovakia.
Purpose Collective defense and military strategy: attack on one is an attack on all. Counter-balance to NATO and to protect security and interests of countries behind Iron Curtain.
Capabilities Approx 50 divisions (25 active). Increased strength agreed in 1952. Approx 5.5 million personnel in 175 divisions, 35,000 tanks, 100,000 aircraft.
Nuclear Weapons Held by USA, Britain (from 1952), France (from 1960). Held by the USSR.
Leadership Collective leadership, though USA provided majority of funds and firepower. Officially collective, but in reality all armed forces controlled by USSR and decisions made in Moscow.
