Command Words & Question Stems Decoder

Compare:

Identify direct similarities (and differences where prompted) across eras or events.

Convincing:

Assess historical accuracy against your own knowledge—what does it get right, exaggerate, or omit?

Describe a problem:

Explain why something was a difficulty or obstacle, supported by precise factual details.

Explain:

Set out clear reasons why or how an outcome occurred using causal connectives.

How far do you agree:

To what extent do you agree or disagree? Construct a balanced argument with a sustained, justified conclusion.

How useful:

To what extent does the source add to a historian's understanding? Evaluate content, provenance (NOP), and limits.

In what ways:

Explore different aspects or areas that changed over time (e.g. social, economic, or groups).

Interpretation:

The historical opinion / perspective of someone looking back on an issue, leader, or period.

Significance:

The historical importance and impact of something in the short term and the long term.

Source:

A piece of historical evidence produced at the time or shortly after (text, photo, cartoon, report).

Write an account:

Explain how a sequence of events unfolded and escalated chronologically from catalyst to outcome.

Structure Decoders (8, 12 & 16 Marks)

PEEL For Explanatory Paragraphs
  • P • Point Sentence: Introduce the clear core argument of your paragraph.
  • E • Evidence: Select precise factual historical knowledge (names, dates, figures).
  • E • Explain: Demonstrate directly how your evidence proves the point sentence.
  • L • Link: Connect your point back to the overall question or final judgement.
JEON For Concluding Paragraphs (12 & 16 Marks)
  • J • Judgement: State your decisive answer to the prompt immediately.
  • E • Evidence: Provide your strongest comparative evidence justifying your choice.
  • O • On the other hand: Acknowledge the significance of the alternative factor.
  • N • Nevertheless: Explain why your chosen factor was ultimately more decisive.

Ingredients for Source-Based Questions

1. Content (What It Says / Shows)

The explicit information and visual details shown in the source. Always make an inference from quotes or images and support it with your own contextual knowledge.

2. Provenance (NOP: Origin & Context)

The origin and background of the source. Examine how these factors shape its utility or viewpoint:

  • Nature: Type of source (e.g. private diary, speech, government report, cartoon).
  • Origin: Author, background, nationality, and date created.
  • Purpose: The aim or motive—designed to persuade, deceive, inform, or record privately?
  • Audience: Who was it created for (public citizens, voters, military, private record)?
